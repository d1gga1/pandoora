Light where it matters.

A tamburato is a wooden panel built like a sandwich — two rigid skins bonded to a light honeycomb core: the surface and strength of a solid board at a fraction of the weight. Three things matter most. It weighs almost nothing. It goes up fast. And, treated well, it can be reused 4–5 times or more — so you don't bin the stand at the end of the show, you take it home and save on the next one.

How a tamburato is made

  1. Outer skins. Two rigid facing boards give the panel its flat, workable surface.
  2. Perimeter frame. A solid wooden frame provides structure and a base for edge-banding.
  3. Honeycomb core. A light lattice bonded to both skins — rigidity without the weight.
  4. Finish & edge. Banded, then painted, lacquered, laminated or mirrored to spec.

Hollow-Core Panels

The building block of the entire system — engineered to your exact dimensions.

The hollow-core panel is the heart of everything Pan.door.a builds. Two rigid outer skins are bonded to a lightweight core and a solid perimeter frame — the flat surface and strength of a solid board at a fraction of the weight.

Structure

Low Walls (Muretti)

Rigid, screw-linked walls built to carry real hanging weight.

Muretti are stood up side by side, exactly like tamburato panels — but they are built to be rigid. Where a standard panel can't take the weight, a run of low walls can.

On each piece the MDF skin overhangs the core slightly on the top and bottom face. Align two muretti and a channel is left between them: a wooden upright the full height of the wall drops into that channel and is screwed from the inside. The two pieces are locked together, and you move on to the next.

They are used above all at Salone del Mobile, where exhibitors hang complete furniture and other heavy objects off the walls. To make the run self-supporting, always build at least one corner — a straight line of muretti on its own will fall.

Features

  • Stand side by side like panels
  • Locked with inner uprights and screws
  • Carry heavy hanging loads
  • Build a corner to stay standing

Panic Exit Door

A real emergency exit, inside a modular hollow-core door case.

When a stand has an enclosed room, a store or a meeting space, it needs an exit that opens on a push — no keys, no thinking. The Pan.door.a panic door is a modular double-leaf case with a horizontal push bar on both leaves and push-to-open action on each.

The case assembles and knocks down like the rest of the system: hollow-core construction, 40 mm leaf thickness, 1180 mm clear opening within a 1400 mm overall width. Leaf height is 2100 mm, with an over-panel taking the assembly up to 4000 mm.

It finishes like any other panel on the stand — raw, laminated or veneered — so the escape route disappears into the wall instead of interrupting it.

How the panels connect

A tool-light system — milled grooves, steel joining plates and screws. Panels lock together flush and rigid, then unscrew cleanly for transport and reuse.

  1. MDF stiffening core (anima). First, a thin MDF strip — the anima — is slid lengthwise into the narrow milled grooves along the panel edges. It braces the hollow core so the panel stands up solid and dead-straight, with no flex once raised vertical.
  2. Plates into the grooves. Steel joining plates drop straight into the milled recesses (fresate) machined into the panel frames. Each plate bridges the joint between adjacent panels, pulling them into perfect alignment — in-line, corner, tee or cross.
  3. Screw-fixed. With the plates seated in the grooves, screws lock them down through the pre-set holes. The wall becomes rigid and gap-free — yet fully demountable for the next show.

Muretti on site — from bare panel to finished stand

A single build, photographed through four phases. Every wall, arch and volume you see is made from the same demountable low-wall panels — nothing here is stick-built on site, and nothing is thrown away at the end of the show.

01 · Bare walls go up

The low walls (muretti) land on the stand floor first — raw hollow-core panels, screwed together into a self-standing shell. At this stage the whole footprint of the stand is already readable: arched openings, room divisions and circulation are all set by the panels themselves, before a single finish is applied.

02 · Cladding and fit-out

With the shell standing, the walls become the substrate. Flooring goes down inside the panel footprint, and sample boards, rails and display panels are fixed directly to the low walls — the hollow-core build takes screws and hanging loads across the full face.

03 · Finishing and light

Joints are filled, faces are finished and the lighting goes in. From this point the panel construction disappears completely — what reads is a continuous, solid, purpose-built interior.

04 · The finished stand

Colour, branding and shaped openings, all on the same demountable low-wall system. After the show the walls unscrew, travel flat and go back up on the next stand.
