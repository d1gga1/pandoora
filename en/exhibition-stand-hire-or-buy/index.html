Stand walls: hire or buy? The real sum

The crossover is not counted in square metres but in builds — and it usually lands at the second or third. Here is how to check it for your case.

The sum is in builds, not in square metres

The question almost always arrives the wrong way round: "what does a square metre of stand wall cost?" Put like that it has no useful answer, because hire and purchase are not paid at the same moment or the same number of times.

The right question is one sentence: how many times will this stand go up?

Hire is paid in full at every show. A purchased set of walls is paid once and then costs freight and storage. The crossover is not an opinion, it is a division — and in our experience of the UK and Irish circuit it usually lands between the second and third build.

What pushes it later, in favour of hire: one show, a long way from base, no storage, or a scheme that changes completely every year. What pulls it earlier, in favour of buying: a recurring show, several shows a year, a height or shape outside the standard grid, or a finish the hire stock does not carry.

There is one UK-specific wrinkle worth adding to the sum. Because Great Britain has a customs step on every consignment, a purchased set that lives in a British store is cleared once. Material that comes over for each show and goes back crosses the border twice, every time. That tilts the arithmetic towards buying rather further than it does on the Continent.

When hire is the right answer — and we will say so

We sell panels, and we regularly tell people to hire. Better to write it down.

Hire if you are exhibiting once. One show, a one-off activation, a market test: buying a set of walls for three days is a poor calculation even at a lower rate per square metre, because you also pay freight, customs and the storage of something you will not use again.

Hire if you have nowhere to store them. A wall set for a 40 m² stand is roughly a pallet and a half, stored on edge in a dry space. Without that space the stock deteriorates between shows and the saving is gone in two seasons.

Hire if the scheme changes completely every time. Then the reusable panel has nothing to reuse.

In those three cases a UK hire company or your stand contractor's own stock will serve you better and faster than we will. For a single small stand, honestly, so will shell scheme.

When buying to size becomes obvious

From the second or third build. The most common case, and it covers most recurring exhibitors: annual trade shows, sector shows, regional shows. After three editions the hire has been paid three times and nothing is left; the purchase was paid once and the stock still exists.

As soon as the height leaves the grid. Hire stock is dimensioned around common heights. If the drawing asks for 4 or 5 metres you are outside the catalogue, and a hired non-standard height often costs more than a bought one. We manufacture up to 6,000 mm in one piece.

As soon as the shape is not straight. A radius, an angle that is not 90°, a particular volume: precisely the cases modular systems price heavily and made-to-size treats as ordinary.

As soon as the finish is part of the argument. A RAL-lacquered panel in your brand colour, a mirror panel, a seamless run: hire stock gives you white, which is perfectly fine — but if the stand has to be recognisable, it has to be made.

As soon as you exhibit more than once a year. Two or three shows in the same year is often enough to pay the stock back in a single season — and on a GB stock, to save a second and third customs clearance.

What actually decides how long a wall set lasts

Buying only makes sense if the panel survives. And that is decided not by the finish but by two construction details you cannot see in a photograph.

The perimeter frame. A honeycomb panel without a finger-jointed timber frame all the way round has nothing to hold a screw and nothing to take a knock on the edge. That is the panel that cups on the third handling. The frame is the difference between a panel that lasts three shows and one that lasts ten years.

How it is joined. The most underrated point in the trade. A panel screwed hard into its neighbour loses material every time it comes apart, and after four builds the edges no longer meet. A panel assembled with I·L·T·X connectors and alignment rails comes apart cleanly indefinitely, because the load goes into the ironmongery rather than the board.

If you are buying in order to reuse — which is the whole point of buying — then the connectors line is not an accessory, it is the protection on the investment. The alignment rail is free with our panels and connectors start at a few euro each: set against the years they add to the stock, it is the most profitable line on the order.

The third factor is not ours: storage. On edge, in a dry space, on bearers — never flat on the floor and never against a damp wall. A well-made, badly stored set does no better than a mediocre one.

The option nobody offers: a mixed stock

It is not either/or, and it is how the contractors who do this well actually work.

Buy what stays, hire what changes. The perimeter walls, the doors, the raised floor: neutral components that serve again whatever this year's concept is. Those you buy. The signage, the scenographic volumes of a campaign, the season's furniture: hire those or make them again.

A stock of white stand walls made to size, with doors and platforms, lasts ten years and carries a different scheme every year — because what changes sits in front of the panel rather than being the panel.

On fire classification, apply the same logic but read the requirement first: in the UK the exhibitor manual may still use legacy Class 1 wording, and what it will actually accept is worth settling in writing before you buy a stock rather than after.

Frequently asked questions

After how many shows does buying pay for itself?

Usually between the second and third build. Hire is paid in full at every show; a bought stock is paid once and then costs only freight and storage. It moves later if the scheme changes completely each year or you have no dry storage, and earlier if you exhibit several times a year. In Great Britain it moves earlier again, because a bought stock held in the UK clears customs once instead of on every trip.

How much storage does a wall set need?

About a pallet and a half for a 40 m² stand, stored on edge in a dry space on bearers — never flat on the floor and never against a damp wall. Without suitable storage, hire remains the better calculation.

How many times can a honeycomb panel be rebuilt?

Dozens of cycles, on two conditions: a finger-jointed timber frame all the way round, and assembly with connectors and alignment rails rather than screws into the neighbouring panel. The joining method decides the life, not the finish.

Can a stock be built up gradually?

Yes. There is no minimum order, single pieces included, and non-standard sizes are charged at the price of the next standard size up. Many customers add a few panels each season rather than funding everything in year one.

Does the customs step change the hire-or-buy sum for the UK?

It tilts it towards buying. A purchased set held in a British store is cleared once. Material that crosses for each show and returns is declared twice per show, every show. Over three or four shows that is a real difference on top of the hire cost itself.
